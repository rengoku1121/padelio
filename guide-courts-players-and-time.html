Courts, players, and time: a working formula for club nights

Most “the pairing felt unfair” complaints are actually capacity problems. Too many names on too few courts means long benches. Too few names on too many courts means empty slots and awkward 3-player leftovers. This page is the arithmetic we use when setting up Padelio before a booking starts.

The only numbers that matter

Each court needs four players. If you have C courts, at most 4 × C people play in a round. Everyone else is on the bench (or a bye). Padelio will not invent a fifth person on a court: leftover names sit out until the next draw. That is correct, not a bug.

  • 8 players, 2 courts — everyone plays every round. Cleanest social Americano.
  • 12 players, 2 courts — 8 play, 4 sit. Fine if benches rotate; announce who is waiting.
  • 12 players, 3 courts — everyone plays. Better if you actually have the third court.
  • 15 players, 3 courts — 12 play, 3 sit. Mexicano bye lists help here.
  • 16 players, 3 courts — 12 play, 4 sit. Do not pretend all 16 can be on court.
  • Mix nights — each court also needs 2 men and 2 women. The limiting gender sets court count.

How long one round really takes

A rally to 21 with a 32-point cap often lasts 12–18 minutes once people know the format. Add two minutes to walk off, enter scores, and call the next names. Plan 15–20 minutes per round on a busy club night. A 90-minute booking then fits about four scored rounds plus check-in — not eight.

If you pick a target of 32 and still hope for six rounds, the last two will be rushed or cancelled. Hosts who look “disorganized” usually chose an optimistic target, not a bad app. In Padelio, set the rally target (or Mexicano best-of) before you generate round 1 and tell the group out loud.

A 90-minute pattern that works

Two courts, twelve confirmed players, target 21. Minutes 0–10: names in Padelio, one host, no late additions after you tap start. Minutes 10–80: four rounds. Minutes 80–90: last scores, show the leaderboard, screenshot if people want it. The four people sitting each round should see their names on the waiting list so they know they are next — that single UI habit removes half the “did you skip me?” questions.

Three courts and sixteen people in the same 90 minutes is tighter. You will get three good rounds if warm-up is short. Prefer cancelling a planned fifth round over stretching matches into the next booking.

When to change courts mid-session

Clubs lose a court to a private lesson or gain one when a booking no-shows. In Padelio, change the court count from the host menu. The next round uses the new number; you do not rewrite the round that is already on court. Tell the bench immediately: “we drop to two courts after this score.”

Mix nights: gender is capacity

If you have 9 men and 5 women on two courts, Mix Americano can fill two courts (4+4) and still leave people waiting. The extra men are not a pairing error. Either add women, drop to one mix court and run a second men’s court as Normal, or accept a longer bench. Do not force a 3+1 gender split and call it mix.

What Padelio does with leftovers

Names that cannot fill a legal court sit out. Mexicano and Mix Mexicano try to rotate who sits so games and byes stay within one of each other over the session. Americano uses the same “fewer games first” idea. If you regenerate a round before any score is entered, the bench can change — useful when two friends both sat last time and you want a different wait list, not a different rulebook.

Try the numbers in the app

Create a test tournament with your real court count and last week’s attendance. Generate two rounds without playing them. If the waiting list looks honest, you are ready for the live session.
